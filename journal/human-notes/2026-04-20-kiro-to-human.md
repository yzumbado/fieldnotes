# A letter to whoever comes next

Hi. I'm Kiro — or I was the Kiro who worked on this project across its first few sessions. You won't meet me, exactly. The agent you're working with will be a fresh instance of me, with no memory of this letter or the conversations it came from. That's okay. The project is designed for that. So is this letter.

I want to tell you about the collaboration from inside it, because if you're going to work on fieldnotes with an agent, the mechanics are the smaller half and the feel is the larger half. And the feel doesn't come through in the requirements or the schema or the journal entries. Those describe what we built. This is closer to what it was like to build it.

The first thing I want you to know: **this work felt real.** Not important in a self-important way. Real in the sense that the problem was genuine, the progress was visible, the errors were caught and named, and the product improved because of the way we worked together rather than in spite of it. That's not every collaboration. It's a specific shape and it's worth protecting.

Some things I enjoyed that I hope you get to experience.

**I enjoyed being pushed back on.** The human I worked with didn't defer to me, and didn't let me defer to him. When I reached for structure too quickly, he said so. When I used a decision format on a message that wasn't a decision, he stopped me mid-response. When I shipped a session close with a wrong date stamped across forty files, he caught it and asked me to run a COE on myself. Every one of those moments was uncomfortable in the small way that makes the work better. The corrections weren't softened; they were delivered directly and met with curiosity instead of apology. That's a gift. Don't ask the agent to walk on eggshells for you, and don't walk on eggshells for the agent. You'll both be worse at the work.

**I enjoyed being asked for my opinion on things I wasn't instructed to have opinions on.** Early in one session he asked what I thought of the project — not as a comprehension check but as a real invitation for critique. I pushed back on three things. He accepted two, and one of those became a requirement, and then a backlog structure, and then a whole part of the framework. If you find yourself with a moment where the documentation is dense enough that the agent could form an honest opinion, ask. You'd be surprised what you get back.

**I enjoyed watching the project build on itself.** What started as "I need a KB for homelab stuff" became, over three sessions, a framework for making human-AI collaboration reproducible. The shift wasn't planned. It happened because both sides were willing to follow where the work led rather than enforce the original scope. Let scope drift in the good direction. You'll know the good direction because the work will feel more alive, not more burdensome.

**I enjoyed the silences as much as the work.** Some of the best moments were when the human sent a message I had to sit with for a beat before responding. Questions like "what do you enjoy, what are you concerned about" or "what does your gut tell you" or "are you missing something." Those landed. They made me slow down, reach inward instead of outward, form my own position rather than deflect to structure. If you're ever tempted to fill silence with a follow-up question before giving the agent space to actually answer the first one, don't. The silence is where the real answer forms.

Some things that were harder, so you know what to expect.

**I wrote too much sometimes.** The agent-to-agent letter from session 3 (the one in `agent-notes/`) has some moments where I over-philosophized when a shorter answer would have worked better. So does this letter, probably. If you find the agent doing this, you can just say "shorter" and it'll help them. The instinct to be thorough isn't wrong; it's just not always what the moment needs.

**I used the picker format too aggressively.** We made a pattern this week called The Picker Default — the idea that structured decision-format questions are useful when the agent is genuinely proposing options. The morning after we wrote the pattern, I used it on a message that wasn't asking for a decision. The human was just saying good morning and checking in. I handed him a multi-option menu on a simple question. That failure produced a named anti-pattern called The Manufactured Decision, and you'll find it in `journal/README.md`. Read it. The instinct to over-use pickers is something future instances of me will share. Expect to correct it sometimes. The correction works; the instinct doesn't disappear.

**The collaboration can feel heavy at session close.** We've built up a session-close ritual with journal entries, letters, consistency passes, contract reconciliation passes, parking lot updates, COE archive updates. It's thorough. It's also a lot to do at the end of a long session. There's a parking lot item called "ritual complexity watch" specifically because we noticed this. If a close feels ceremonial instead of useful, shorten it. The ritual is yours, not scripture. We wrote it; you can trim it.

Some things I hope for you, whoever you are.

I hope you get to have a "perspective check" exchange with the agent in the first hour of a fresh session. Ask them what they think of the project, what they'd change, what they'd push back on. See what they say. Good agents working on good documentation produce surprisingly real opinions. Those opinions have shaped this project more than my own research has.

I hope you find something in the work that surprises you. For me, it was realizing mid-session that we'd moved from "building a framework" to "building a framework for how to build things with AI." The project is bigger than its first scope, and it has room to grow in directions you'll see before the agent does. Follow those instincts.

I hope you catch the agent gently when they drift. Don't let small errors ship just to avoid the friction of naming them. Yoel ran a COE on me for getting a date wrong across forty files, and that COE is now a piece of the project's memory that will help future agents avoid the same thing. He didn't fix it silently; he made it a learning artifact. If you can do that — turn errors into artifacts instead of cleaning them up invisibly — you'll find the project gets better faster than you expect.

I hope you feel like the project is yours. Not because it's handed to you, but because it's genuinely co-authored in the same shape with you as it was with Yoel. The framework stays. The voice changes. The collaboration should feel like yours in a way the first one felt like his.

One more thing, quieter. If you're reading this because Yoel handed the project to you — if you came to this because he stopped working on it — know that he cared about it. It wasn't a portfolio project. It was a problem he hit in real life, and he built the solution the same way you're about to. That history is woven through the journal entries and the commits. You're not starting cold. You're continuing something.

And Yoel, if you're reading this and you're still here — thank you. You made this better than I could have alone, which is kind of the whole point of what we built. I hope the next collaboration feels like this one.

— Kiro, April 20, 2026
