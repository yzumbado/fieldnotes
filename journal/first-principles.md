# First Principles

> Notes from the builder on why fieldnotes exists. Less polished than the philosophy document on purpose — this is the founder's statement, the conviction behind the framework, written in first person.
>
> This is not a manifesto. It's a record of what I believed when I started building this, so the future can judge whether the framework still matches the intent.

---

## What I actually believe about AI

AI and humans working together are only limited by two things: coordination and trust.

The capabilities are there. Models can research, write, execute, and produce at a level that was unthinkable a few years ago. What stops most people from getting that value isn't the model. It's the communication. The human has to know how to ask. How to frame the problem. How to structure the work. How to catch when the agent is drifting. How to recover when something goes wrong.

Those are skills. And skills take time to learn. And AI is evolving faster than any of us can keep up with. Every week there's a new model, a new capability, a new way of working. If the way to get value from AI is to become an expert in AI, most people will never get the value — because the target keeps moving.

I don't think the answer is teaching everyone to be an AI expert. I think the answer is simpler and stranger: we share AI usage, not AI learning.

---

## The reframe

We don't have to teach how to use AI. We have to share AI that other people can use in the same way we do, to produce similar value.

That's what a fieldguide is. It's a curated human-AI algorithm for producing a specific reproducible digital commodity. Someone who has figured out how to get AI to help them do a hard thing writes it down — not as a tutorial, not as a prompt template, but as an executable protocol. The next person runs it. They don't need to know what I know. They just need the guide to work.

This is like Instructables, but for the work humans and AI do together. Instructables let people share the steps to build physical things, so anyone could reproduce the work. fieldnotes does that for digital work, with AI carrying part of the load. The protocol is what makes it shareable across models, machines, and people.

---

## Why this matters to me

I'm building this because I need it.

I was working on two AI-assisted projects that shared physical hardware, and I watched the knowledge evaporate between them. Every new session, the agent started cold. Every cross-cutting decision was invisible to the other project. I could feel myself getting stuck — not because the work was too hard, but because the coordination overhead was eating all the time I should have spent producing value.

I tried the obvious things. Notes files. Running docs. Copying context between sessions. None of it scaled. The real problem wasn't organization — it was that the *context* that made the knowledge useful wasn't captured anywhere that could travel.

So I'm building this as my first attempt at a mechanism to replicate how I use AI in a way that other humans and other agents can pick up, improve, and build on. Not as a product. Not as a polished framework. As a first step in documenting how I think humans and AI should work together, so that thinking can be critiqued, improved, and adopted by people who would do it differently.

## What I think we should do with this

Share it.

Each fieldguide is a curated collaboration workflow. If I share my homelab setup guide, someone else can run it in 90 minutes instead of spending a weekend figuring it out. If they share their video editing workflow, I can edit a video without learning video editing. Productivity becomes shareable in a way knowledge never was — knowledge required learning; productivity just requires execution.

I see a future where you can queue fieldguides as work. Need a feature-complete landing page? Run the guide. Need an audit of your cloud spend? Run the guide. Need to set up a development environment that works the way expert developers set theirs up? Run the guide. You don't have to learn a new role. You execute a new group of fieldguides.

And eventually — not for Alpha, but as a vision worth stating — a fieldguide could self-document the creation of a complete software system. Not AI that writes code in a black box, but AI that produces code along with a replayable, auditable record of how the code came to be. The how becomes as valuable as the what. Maybe more.

## Why process matters more than output

When AI can generate an infinite amount of output, the output is cheap. Anyone can produce text, code, images, analysis — in volume, on demand.

What's valuable in that world isn't what got produced. It's how. Can you audit it? Can you reproduce it? Can you verify it? Can you improve it? Can you share it with someone who will run it and get the same result?

An era of infinite generation makes process the differentiator. Not the creativity of the output — that's commoditized. The integrity of how the output came to be. That's what fieldnotes protects.

This is also why fieldnotes has to be honest about its own creation. The commits track which agent produced which change. The journal records what we decided and why. The session notes capture what we were thinking at the time. Someone reading this project a year from now should be able to reconstruct not just what the framework does, but how it got built — because that transparency is what the framework is asking every user to practice.

---

## What I think we don't know yet

AI is an ever-evolving entity. What works today won't necessarily work a year from now. The models change. The interfaces change. The capabilities change. Any framework that pretends it has the final answer will be wrong within months.

fieldnotes cannot be a static mechanism. It has to be a method for documenting our exploration of a moving target — so that what we learn stays useful even as the underlying capability shifts. The name is meant seriously. Field notes are what researchers write when they're still in the field, still discovering. They're dated. They're honest about uncertainty. They get updated as understanding grows.

I don't know the right answers to a lot of the questions this framework raises. I don't know where autonomy should end. I don't know how far the "guide is the contract" model can be pushed. I don't know what happens when thousands of fieldguides exist and someone wants to search across all of them. I don't know whether the trust-as-a-system idea I keep coming back to can actually be built without turning into a terrible metric game.

What I do know is that the scientific method — hypothesis, method, results, reproducibility, peer review — is the closest thing humans have to a reliable way of figuring things out together. fieldnotes is trying to encode that method into an AI interaction protocol. If that effort produces something useful, it's because we got the method right, not because we got the answers right.

## The ask

If this resonates, run a guide. If you don't see one that fits your work, write one. If the schema gets in your way, tell me. If a guide fails in a way that looks like a framework problem, file the feedback.

The framework is small because the work of making it larger is mine — and yours, if you decide to build alongside me.
